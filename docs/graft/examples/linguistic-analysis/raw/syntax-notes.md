# Notes: syntax and constituency

Syntax studies how words combine into phrases and sentences. A
constituent is a group of words that behaves as a unit.

Constituency tests:

- Substitution: a constituent can be replaced by a pro-form
  ("the old dog" → "it").
- Movement: constituents can be fronted ("That book, I never read").
- Coordination: only like constituents conjoin with "and".

Phrase structure: each phrase has a head that determines its category —
noun phrase (NP), verb phrase (VP), prepositional phrase (PP),
adjective phrase (AP). A simple clause pairs an NP subject with a VP
predicate: S → NP VP.

Trees represent hierarchical structure. Structural ambiguity arises
when one string has two trees: "old men and women" can group
[old men] and [women] or old [men and women].

Grammatical relations: the subject typically precedes the verb in
English; the direct object follows it. English is an SVO language.
Recursion lets phrases contain phrases of the same type ("the house on
the hill by the river"), so a finite grammar generates infinitely many
sentences.
