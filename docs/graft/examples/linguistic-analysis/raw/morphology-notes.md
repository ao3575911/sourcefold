# Notes: morphology

Morphology studies the internal structure of words. The morpheme is
the smallest meaningful unit of language. Free morphemes stand alone
as words (dog, run); bound morphemes must attach to another form
(un-, -ness, -s).

Affix types: prefixes attach before a stem, suffixes after. English
uses both; infixes and circumfixes occur in other languages.

Derivational morphology creates new lexemes, often changing category:
happy → happiness (adjective → noun). Inflectional morphology marks
grammatical features without changing category: walk → walked (tense),
cat → cats (number). English has a small inflectional inventory of
eight regular inflectional suffixes.

Morphological analysis segments a word into morphemes and labels each:
un-help-ful-ness = negative prefix + root + adjectivizer + nominalizer.

Allomorphy: one morpheme with several realizations. The English plural
appears as /s/ (cats), /z/ (dogs), and /əz/ (horses), conditioned by
the final sound of the stem.
