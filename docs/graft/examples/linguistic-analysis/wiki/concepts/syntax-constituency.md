# Syntax and constituency

Syntax studies how words combine into phrases and sentences. A
**constituent** is a group of words that behaves as a unit.

## Constituency tests

- **Substitution:** a constituent can be replaced by a pro-form
  ("the old dog" → "it").
- **Movement:** constituents can be fronted ("That book, I never
  read").
- **Coordination:** only like constituents conjoin with "and".

## Phrase structure

Each phrase has a **head** that determines its category — noun phrase
(NP), verb phrase (VP), prepositional phrase (PP), adjective phrase
(AP). A simple clause pairs an NP subject with a VP predicate:
S → NP VP.

Trees represent hierarchical structure. **Structural ambiguity**
arises when one string has two trees: "old men and women" can group
[old men] and [women] or old [men and women].

In English the subject typically precedes the verb and the direct
object follows it — English is an SVO language. **Recursion** lets
phrases contain phrases of the same type ("the house on the hill by
the river"), so a finite grammar generates infinitely many sentences.

What these structures mean is the domain of
[semantics and pragmatics](semantics-pragmatics.md); word-internal
structure is covered under [morphology](morphology.md).
